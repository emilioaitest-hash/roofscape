# 0009 — A building looks like what it has become

**Decided.** A building's drawn form is a function of its headcount. It begins as
a shack and ends as a skyscraper, passing through four intermediate forms, and
the change is automatic — nobody chooses their building's appearance.

    1 floor    a shack             corrugated, patched, leaning
    2 floors   a single storey     pitched roof, a door, two windows
    3–4        a brick walk-up     flat cornice, rows of sash windows
    5–7        a cast-iron block   SoHo: arched bays, a fire escape, ornament
    8–11       a skyscraper        setbacks, a crown
    12+        a landmark          the crown gains a spire

**Why.** The skyline is the product's home screen, and a home screen should tell
you something true at a glance. Height already encodes headcount; form encodes it
again, in a way that reads from across the room and without counting. A shack and
a cast-iron block are different *kinds* of thing, not just different sizes, which
is the honest signal: a one-agent experiment and a seven-agent operation are not
the same undertaking and should not look alike.

It is also the reward. Watching a shack become a brick walk-up because the work
justified two more hires is the part of this that makes people come back, and a
tool nobody opens does nothing however correct it is.

**Why these forms.** They are a real architectural progression — the actual
sequence a growing American commercial district went through, from timber shack
to cast-iron loft to steel frame. Borrowing a true sequence rather than inventing
one means the intermediate steps already look right together.

**Cost.** Six forms to draw and maintain, in a terminal and later in the
dashboard, and they must read at small sizes and in both light and dark. The
thresholds are also arbitrary at the edges — a four-floor building being a
walk-up while a five-floor one is cast-iron is a line drawn somewhere. Both
accepted: the alternative is a list of project names, which tells you nothing.
